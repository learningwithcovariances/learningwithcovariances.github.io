About

Overview

Covariance matrices capture the redundancies between the features of a dataset: brain regions that activate together, stock prices that fluctuate in tandem, traffic volumes that are correlated across intersections. They are a cornerstone of multivariate data analysis, most prominently through principal component analysis (PCA). Yet PCA is a fixed, task-agnostic linear transformation, and learning from sample covariance matrices raises four key challenges: how to use covariance information for representation learning on a downstream task; whether inference is stable to the finite-sample perturbations of the estimated covariance; whether models are transferable across datasets that describe the same phenomenon with a different number of features; and how to make learning interpretable.

This tutorial treats the covariance matrix as a relational inductive bias, a data-driven graph, and introduces coVariance neural networks (VNNs): graph neural networks that operate on sample covariance matrices. Starting from the connection between PCA and the graph Fourier transform, we show that coVariance filters implicitly perform PCA-based inference without computing eigenvectors, and that, unlike PCA, VNNs are provably stable to covariance estimation errors and transferable across scales. We then show how these properties enable principled, anatomically interpretable brain age gap prediction in network neuroscience, and we illustrate how using pairwise statistics as inductive biases underpins a broader set of data processing methods beyond VNNs, including sparse VNNs, precision neural networks and covariance scattering transforms.

Topics covered

  1. PCA and the graph Fourier transform PCA-based inference implemented as a polynomial graph filter over the covariance matrix.
  2. coVariance neural networks (VNNs) coVariance filters as convolutional operators, and VNNs as their layered composition with nonlinearities.
  3. Theory of VNNs: stability and transferability Robustness to finite-sample covariance estimates, scale-free models and graphon limits.
  4. Application: principled brain age gap prediction with VNNs Anatomically interpretable and explainable biomarkers of neurodegeneration.
  5. Variants of VNNs Sparse VNNs, precision neural networks and covariance scattering transforms.

Key takeaways

  • The covariance matrix can serve as a relational inductive bias for representation learning. It improves generalization and parameter efficiency compared with standard neural networks, and stability and transferability compared with PCA.
  • VNNs address methodological and conceptual obscurities in brain age gap prediction.
